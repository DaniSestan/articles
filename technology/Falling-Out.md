Falling Out: A Self-Reminder of Corporate Disruption in IT

Corporate disruptions are inevitable and, at times, necessary to make some definitive impact at a company. They are 
employees' best option for going quid-pro-quo with investments happening behind the scenes of an organization. 
Nonchalant, poorly executed, money-first investments can impact any employee's career trajectory, regardless of what 
they have personally invested through nerves, time and finances of their own. I am happy to admit I was part of a 
corporate disruption inspiring me to do several things: make use of my funds for a sabbatical, opt out of a Computer 
Science graduate program at Georgia Tech and compartmentalize the overtime of my previous job before bringing 
my work portfolio to a new position. This article doesn't request sympathy from the reader; it only offers the 
perspective of the writer. I, personally, intend to read this article as a self-reminder of the warning 
signs indicating when outside investments start taking their toll on internal work through a chain reaction. 

What are some of the visceral experiences resulting from a corporate disruption?
The situation starts off with work becoming ever slightly more tedious. One of the main reasons for this is the 
technical documentation, which can be drier than brisket charred to a crisp on the barbeque. The rule of thumb is 
"Read the Manual". There might be bonus points like a carrot tied to a string for those who "Read the Official 
Manual", also known as the 
source of truth. Programs are,
in fact, a 
form of technical documentation. They are the source of truth for how hardware, physical or virtual, runs a set of 
instructions compiled on an operating system. The 
internet,
itself, is the source of truth 
for how _it_ works. When someone sets aside all the fantastic content the web has to offer only to pull back the hood, 
what they might be startled to find is a technology that resembles a mindless robot programmed to enforce communication 
protocols. There's nothing like seeing this kind of startled reaction from management. It's a cue signaling a 
corporate disruption is much more probable. 

One could even read some tech article or white paper describing startling reactions to 
the internet that 
can occur at 
one of the 
Internet and Cable Services 
providers that handled,
at 
one time, roughly about 1/3 of the internet traffic flowing throughout the United States. Specifically, these things 
can happen 
during the aftermath of a major security breach. Such an incident jeopardized an AWS enterprise level subscription 
costing,
between production and staging Cloud Infrastructure resources, a total of about $100 million and the process of 
shutting down and migrating well over 300 AWS accounts within the organization. I don't need to read articles or 
white papers to understand the impact and the buildup to corporate disruption. I, 
myself, was one of the lucky individuals asked to work overtime to clean up the 
technical mess of that security breach, while adapting to continuously failing work 
processes enforced through the organization's panic.

During the mitigation, it was determined the accounts had been set up following the official AWS documentation 
and consulting guidelines. At this point, company discussions began to veer towards migrating cloud resources to an 
altogether different platform. The company opted not to go through with the option, instead signing off on business 
arrangements to sponsor an AWS branding campaign. As a result, there was a re-org of management, while those and 
other lead positions were to be filled by AWS employees and consultants involved in creating the technical processes 
that, unfortunately, continued to fail the organization. One thing begets another, and this inevitably spurred a chain 
reaction. New and re-branded AWS-centric projects were unable to gain traction or lead to improved results once they 
got off the ground. The first things a network operations department notices are site reliability issues. Any 
progress specific to maintaining network connectivity began to falter, only making the situation more complicated. 
Despite the silver linings of cloud infrastructure that a major retailer such as Amazon can offer, 
it is simply not logical for those services to be more relevant than computer networking itself in a NetOps 
department. It is only possible to trust any individuals accepting lead positions have opted to 
understand networking fundamentals until trust is broken. Those completing networking-specific tasks 
don't need the additional work or training for their 
portfolio to hold more water in such scenarios. 
Anyone else, on the other hand, 
can 
only  
decide 
to invest personal time outside the bounds of their work requirements to reach a similar point. What should be clear 
is how  
CompNet admins, engineers and architects can opt to do overtime of their own, which can leave anyone who wants their 
context for the work between a pile of 
rocks and a 
dry place without a technical well in sight, particularly when things go off the rails.

This was just the kind of bind AWS leads at the company found themselves entangled in. They started to express their 
frustration through incessantly demanding the CompNet admins offer what typically results from years of 
internal knowledge, overtime or some combination of both. Any trust of work processes officially started to wane off 
and 
meetings only became more implosive. This was the start of corporate disruption: A break in trust as 
opposed to the breach itself, which was only the catalyst to problems that ensued. 

Employees, one by one, started turning in their resignations. Anyone remaining who was hired to work on software 
applications was siloed off into FinOps side projects. I, myself, opted to stay on while reviewing the original 
software project halted during the mitigation. Up until that point, the app had been a thoughtful exercise of Python 
Flask's capabilities as a framework hosted on a cloud computing platform. The code was modularized enough to build 
from, branching into what I felt could align with the team's main priorities. An enterprise cloud computing 
subscription at a major organization is unique in how it involves thousands of associated requests for provisioning, 
maintaining and decommissioning accounts. A web services app layered over GitOps pipeline stages could be its own 
framework, servicing requests for provisioning and decommissioning accounts. Meanwhile, the process to maintain 
the configuration settings -- within the scope of account templates -- could continue to be part of regular support 
operations. Not only is Python the definitive language used to make AWS API calls, Python-based apps offer the best 
plug for business intelligence and data analytics. This could have given the team an opportunity to "sell out" the 
completed project, provided departments in those areas wanted to make use of the metrics involved with account 
creation and deletion. As opposed to creating a project with a simple starting point and practical framework, 
arguments about creating a React app from scratch to handle chained back-and-forth requests about account 
provisioning began to bounce off the walls, turning meeting rooms into echo chambers of nonsense. Anyone with a keen 
eye for work processes through overtime understands handling chained threads between department leads and their 
possible escalations is management's job. The motive behind their app pitch was to make work requirements for 
management less involved. Their proposal for building it pushed for technologies that did not make use of existing 
project work, extended the duration of the project timelines through additional complexities and, most importantly, 
was not backed by the initiative to create a software product offering services of the most value on the team: 
the support ops work itself. Such an app went against what I find most effective when evaluating 
software. When 
I work on support operations teams, I don't "steal" from their work. I've opted to participate through their support 
work to understand how what they're doing can be of value. Software is just supposed to be the next best 
thing compared to someone manually working on anything. Those concerned with apps taking their jobs often ignore the 
proposals for such software because they fail to understand how better apps lighten the load on support, offering 
them the opportunity to work on advanced manual tasks. 

What sealed off my decision to opt out of any continued involvement with the company was a result of its 
investments with Amazon in the aftermath of the breach. As a result of what became an AWS rebranding campaign, it 
was announced to the team that 1. the only form of accepted training would be AWS certifications and 2. certificate 
completion was no longer to be incentivized by the org.
An AWS certificate was of lesser value to my career 
than the graduate courses I invested my time in. I enrolled in a Computer Science program at Georgia Tech with two 
things in mind:
A. It could supplement my understanding of Software and Computer Networking fundamentals during my time learning 
about Cloud Infrastructure on the job.
B. It was the best option to receive permanent credit for any additional training as overtime.

Since leaving my previous position, what has changed is my openness to ever trust how an organization values 
personal career investments. I am no longer accepting any requests for additional career training. I've gone through 
my rounds of additional requests for job training through an Associate's in Computer Networking, a 
Bachelor's in IT and the courses completed in a Computer Science graduate program. Certifications are targeted at 
brand consultants, offering a basic overview of what products and services can offer. 

What hasn't changed?
I sleep well at night on the topic of my work portfolio. I accept opportunities aligning with my experience in a 
software framework or that offer work through tech companies creating products and services relevant to my career. I 
am continuing to accept full-time software engineering positions. Despite the fact that Development Operations roles 
may offer some 
form of software development work, I am opting out of those roles. Having worked on DevOps tasks for several 
years, I have reached the same conclusion about the work
as most people by now: the requirements are mostly influenced by a technical product as opposed to the needs of the 
IT industry. When companies subscribe to web services, they basically buy into an abstraction between their own IT 
services and the computing infrastructure necessary for IT operations. Amazon began expanding as a retailer with 
attempts to corner the market on web services several years ago. When a company subscribes to AWS, Amazon typically 
targets those specializing in small scale software projects in Support Ops groups. This is to influence their 
choices for automating operations over time. For instance, HashiCorp's Terraform might be better suited for 
processes involving Infrastructure as Code. AWS' official support and guidelines are aimed to dispute this, offering 
advice on what's offered through web services as the best option first. Some work scenarios may decide to bundle 
everything into one platform. This doesn't necessarily improve the quality of the work or prevent those assigned to 
software development tasks from only understanding technology through the lens of a product missionary. "They're 
offering me and anyone else little blue pills and steak dinners in a McDonald's to-go box to push these services" 
becomes a common complaint. Those things aren't enough for me to limit my understanding of what can be created through 
technology. I never expect coworkers to respect my _personal_ perspective on the job. I only ask individuals to 
respect the context I've earned working through technology. When this becomes something to demand, my job 
requirement just becomes collecting any software I've worked on for the purpose of trading it out at another company.
Before, I was once bothered by the phrase "An employee's feelings only hinder work progress". Now, it's more obvious 
how those involved in the IT industry with the privilege of making the phrase a known motto during 
corporate disruptions, security breaches and other technical issues, officially have fewer 
requirements for implementing the technology. My kind of work is not the same as theirs and I never want it to be. 
My work is only to set the conditions for success, in the software I write and the work conditions I sign off on.

Architecting those wonderful machines that do the computing is work spanning over the time and region of any 
civilization. Programming the logic of what they can calculate is nothing new either. So, what is new about computer 
science? What's different is the formal recognition of in most academic institutes. Common logic says that the basic 
progression of learning starts with reading, moving into writing, ending up with arithmetic. Forced pedantics aren't 
required to teach anyone how the mind works. What, then, is the point of learning from any institution? 

Institutions have a history of archiving what is known about the world by housing what's been published about it. 
Some institutions even have an unfortunate history of not investing in the resources to back up their archives. This 
is colloquially referred to as "chaining the books". The term is based on the factual practice of chaining books to 
libraries, much like how academic publications are made accessible to readers through membership only. If one were 
to estimate the value of a precious metal on one of those chain links, multiplying it by the number of those links 
in the chain, someone could quickly determine the cost of transcribing the 
book 
onto fresh parchment. One or more books can be available to readers, while one of the books is retained as an 
archival backup, removing the impractical excuse for chaining the book to a shelf.
Translating it 
is even more costly. Translation is a form of linguistic work with extended requirements. Technically, a scribe doesn't 
need to be literate. They can just attempt to create an exact replica, much like someone can paint a landscape 
they've never seen by looking at any photocopy or still life image. Someone with context for the language requires 
less time to transcribe what they've read. Chaining books is a matter that blocks trade to some capacity by limiting 
the options to exchange information. At any point throughout history, there are going to be academics that are 
locked into mental institutions by any other name, reading from books that are not accessible from outside the 
facility. What perpetuates this problem is often times the people who think attaining membership to a book chaining 
club 
is going to fix all their problems. "Society won't let me join a mental institution." Personally, I opt out of 
entertaining such a statement, whether it's made by me or anyone else.
