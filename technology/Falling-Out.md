# Falling Out: A Self-Reminder of Corporate Disruption in IT

#### Dania Sestan

###### 08/11/2026

&emsp;Corporate disruptions are inevitable and, at times, necessary to make some definitive impact at a company. They 
are employees' best option for going quid-pro-quo with investments happening behind the scenes of an organization. Nonchalant, poorly executed, money-first investments can impact any employee's career trajectory, regardless of what they have personally invested through nerves, time and finances of their own. I am happy to admit I was part of a corporate disruption inspiring me to do several things: make use of my funds for a sabbatical, opt out of a Computer Science graduate program at Georgia Tech and compartmentalize the overtime of my previous job before bringing my work portfolio to a new position. This article doesn't request sympathy from the reader; it only offers the perspective of the writer. I, personally, intend to read this article as a self-reminder of the warning signs indicating when outside investments start taking their toll on internal work through a chain reaction. 

&emsp;What are some of the visceral experiences resulting from a corporate disruption? The situation starts off with work becoming ever slightly more tedious. One of the main reasons for this is the technical documentation, which can be drier than brisket charred to a crisp on the barbeque. The rule of thumb is "Read the Manual". There might be bonus points like a carrot tied to a string for those who "Read the Official Manual", also known as the source of truth. Programs are, in fact, a form of technical documentation. They are the source of truth for how hardware, physical or virtual, runs a set of instructions compiled on an operating system. The internet, itself, is the source of truth for how _it_ works. When someone sets aside all the fantastic content the web has to offer only to pull back the hood, what they might be startled to find is a technology that resembles a mindless robot programmed to enforce communication protocols. There's nothing like seeing this kind of startled reaction from management. It's a cue signaling a corporate disruption is much more probable.  One could even read some tech article or white paper describing startling reactions to the internet that can occur at one of the Internet and Cable Services providers that handled, at one time, roughly about 1/3 of the internet traffic flowing throughout the United States. Specifically, these things can happen during the aftermath of a major security breach. Such an incident jeopardized an AWS enterprise level subscription costing, between production and staging Cloud Infrastructure resources, a total of about $100 million and the process of shutting down and migrating well over 300 AWS accounts within the organization. I don't need to read articles or white papers to understand the impact and the buildup to corporate disruption. I, myself, was one of the lucky individuals asked to work overtime to clean up the technical mess of that security breach, while adapting to continuously failing work processes enforced through the organization's panic. 

&emsp;During the mitigation, it was determined the accounts had been set up following the official AWS documentation and consulting guidelines. At this point, company discussions began to veer towards migrating cloud resources to an altogether different platform. The company opted not to go through with the option, instead signing off on business arrangements to sponsor an AWS branding campaign. As a result, there was a re-org of management, while those and other lead positions were to be filled by AWS employees and consultants involved in creating the technical processes that, unfortunately, continued to fail the organization. One thing begets another, and this inevitably spurred a chain reaction. New and re-branded AWS-centric projects were unable to gain traction or lead to improved results once they got off the ground. The first things a network operations department notices are site reliability issues. Any progress specific to maintaining network connectivity began to falter, only making the situation more complicated. Despite the silver linings of cloud infrastructure that a major retailer such as Amazon can offer, it is simply not logical for those services to be more relevant than computer networking itself in a NetOps department. It is only possible to trust any individuals accepting lead positions have opted to understand networking fundamentals until trust is broken. Those completing networking-specific tasks don't need the additional work or training for their portfolio to hold more water in such scenarios. Anyone else, on the other hand, can only decide to invest personal time outside the bounds of their work requirements to reach a similar point. What should be clear is how  CompNet admins, engineers and architects can opt to do overtime of their own, which can leave anyone who wants their context for the work between a pile of rocks and a dry place without a technical well in sight, particularly when things go off the rails. 

&emsp;This was just the kind of bind AWS leads at the company found themselves entangled in. They started to express their frustration through incessantly demanding the CompNet admins offer what typically results from years of internal knowledge, overtime or some combination of both. Any trust of work processes officially started to wane off and meetings only became more implosive. This was the start of corporate disruption: A break in trust as opposed to the breach itself, which was only the catalyst to problems that ensued.  Employees, one by one, started turning in their resignations. Anyone remaining who was hired to work on software applications was siloed off into FinOps side projects. I, myself, opted to stay on while reviewing the original software project halted during the mitigation. Up until that point, the app had been a thoughtful exercise of Python Flask's capabilities as a framework hosted on a cloud computing platform. The code was modularized enough to build from, branching into what I felt could align with the team's main priorities. An enterprise cloud computing subscription at a major organization is unique in how it involves thousands of associated requests for provisioning, maintaining and decommissioning accounts. A web services app layered over GitOps pipeline stages could be its own framework, servicing requests for provisioning and decommissioning accounts. Meanwhile, the process to maintain the configuration settings -- within the scope of account templates -- could continue to be part of regular support operations. Not only is Python the definitive language used to make AWS API calls, Python-based apps offer the best plug for business intelligence and data analytics. This could have given the team an opportunity to "sell out" the completed project, provided departments in those areas wanted to make use of the metrics involved with account creation and deletion. As opposed to creating a project with a simple starting point and practical framework, arguments about creating a React app from scratch to handle chained back-and-forth requests about account provisioning began to bounce off the walls, turning meeting rooms into echo chambers of nonsense. Anyone with a keen eye for work processes through overtime understands handling chained threads between department leads and their possible escalations is management's job. The motive behind their app pitch was to make work requirements for management less involved. Their proposal for building it pushed for technologies that did not make use of existing project work, extended the duration of the project timelines through additional complexities and, most importantly, was not backed by the initiative to create a software product offering services of the most value on the team: the support ops work itself. Such an app went against what I find most effective when evaluating software. When I work on support operations teams, I don't "steal" from their work. I've opted to participate through their support work to understand how what they're doing can be of value. Software is just supposed to be the next best thing compared to someone manually working on anything. Those concerned with apps taking their jobs often ignore the proposals for such software because they fail to understand how better apps lighten the load on support, offering them the opportunity to work on advanced manual tasks. 

&emsp;What sealed off my decision to opt out of any continued involvement with the company was a result of its investments with Amazon in the aftermath of the breach. As a result of what became an AWS rebranding campaign, it was announced to the team that 1. the only form of accepted training would be AWS certifications and 2. certificate completion was no longer to be incentivized by the org. An AWS certificate was of lesser value to my career than the graduate courses I invested my time in. I enrolled in a Computer Science program at Georgia Tech with two things in mind: A. It could supplement my understanding of Software and Computer Networking fundamentals during my time learning about Cloud Infrastructure on the job. B. It was the best option to receive permanent credit for any additional training as overtime. Since leaving my previous position, what has changed is my openness to ever trust how an organization values personal career investments. I am no longer accepting any requests for additional career training. I've gone through my rounds of additional requests for job training through an Associate's in Computer Networking, a Bachelor's in IT and the courses completed in a Computer Science graduate program. Certifications are targeted at brand consultants, offering a basic overview of what products and services can offer. 

&emsp;What hasn't changed? I sleep well at night on the topic of my work portfolio. I accept opportunities aligning with my experience in a software framework or that offer work through tech companies creating products and services relevant to my career. I am continuing to accept full-time software engineering positions. Despite the fact that Development Operations roles may offer some form of software development work, I am opting out of those roles. Having worked on DevOps tasks for several years, I have reached the same conclusion about the work as most people by now: the requirements are mostly influenced by a technical product as opposed to the needs of the IT industry. When companies subscribe to web services, they basically buy into an abstraction between their own IT services and the computing infrastructure necessary for IT operations. Amazon began expanding as a retailer with attempts to corner the market on web services several years ago. When a company subscribes to AWS, Amazon typically targets those specializing in small scale software projects in Support Ops groups. This is to influence their choices for automating operations over time. For instance, HashiCorp's Terraform might be better suited for processes involving Infrastructure as Code. AWS' official support and guidelines are aimed to dispute this, offering advice on what's offered through web services as the best option first. Some work scenarios may decide to bundle everything into one platform. This doesn't necessarily improve the quality of the work or prevent those assigned to software development tasks from only understanding technology through the lens of a product missionary. "They're offering me and anyone else little blue pills and steak dinners in a McDonald's to-go box to push these services" becomes a common complaint. Those things aren't enough for me to limit my understanding of what can be created through technology. I never expect coworkers to respect my _personal_ perspective on the job. I only ask individuals to respect the context I've earned working through technology. When this becomes something to demand, my job requirement just becomes collecting any software I've worked on for the purpose of trading it out at another company. Before, I was once bothered by the phrase "An employee's feelings only hinder work progress". Now, it's more obvious how those involved in the IT industry with the privilege of making the phrase a known motto during corporate disruptions, security breaches and other technical issues, officially have fewer requirements for implementing the technology. My kind of work is not the same as theirs and I never want it to be. My work is only to set the conditions for success, in the software I write and the work conditions I sign off on.
